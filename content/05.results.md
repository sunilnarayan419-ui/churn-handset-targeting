## Results

### Handset age marks a sharp step in churn

Churn is {{ results.eda.churn_le300 }} among customers whose handset is at most 300 days old (n = {{ results.eda.n_le300 }}) and {{ results.eda.churn_301_400 }} for 301 to 400 days (n = {{ results.eda.n_301_400 }}), a difference of about 20 percentage points (chi-square test, p < 0.001) (Figure 1). The step is not a tenure artefact: within customers of at most 12 months the rate moves from {{ results.eda.strata_t1 }}, within 13 to 24 months from {{ results.eda.strata_t2 }}, and beyond 24 months from {{ results.eda.strata_t3 }} (Figure 2). Revenue, usage trend and care calls matter less: across their groups churn ranges only over {{ results.eda.range_revenue }}, {{ results.eda.range_usage }} and {{ results.eda.range_care }} respectively (handset price: {{ results.eda.range_price }}). The 301 to 400 day cohort is {{ results.eda.cohort_share_base }} of customers but holds only {{ results.eda.cohort_share_churners }} of churners, so the rule alone is not selective. These are associations, not evidence that handset age causes churn.

The model concentrates risk far better than the handset-age rule, but at a realistic base rate the large majority of contacted customers would have stayed anyway. Predictive importance is led by customer tenure and handset age, followed by the change in minutes of use and total minutes; this shows what the model uses, not what would change behaviour.
