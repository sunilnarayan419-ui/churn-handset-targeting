## Abstract

**Background.** Large US wireless carriers lose roughly one percent of postpaid customers a month [@tag:tmobile-q2-2026; @tag:verizon-q2-2026-cxdive; @tag:att-q2-2026-thedesk]. A retention programme must therefore find the few customers who are about to leave and contact them cheaply.

**Data and methods.** We analyse {{ results.n_customers }} anonymised customers with {{ results.n_features }} usage, billing and handset fields; {{ results.sample_churn }} churned within 31 to 60 days of the observation date, so the sample is heavily enriched with churners. We fit a gradient-boosting classifier on a stratified 80/20 split, calibrate its probabilities, and rescale them to a realistic one-percent base rate. A transparent break-even model and a sample-size calculation for a randomised hold-out pilot follow from the model.

**Results.** Churn is {{ results.eda.churn_le300 }} for customers whose handset is at most 300 days old and {{ results.eda.churn_301_400 }} for 301 to 400 days, a step that appears in every tenure group. The model reaches a test ROC-AUC of {{ results.gb.test_auc }} (95% bootstrap interval {{ results.gb.auc_lo }} to {{ results.gb.auc_hi }}) against 0.500 for a no-skill baseline. At a 1% base rate the top 5% of customers by risk are {{ results.tgt.top5.lift }} as likely to churn as average, but about 95% of them would have stayed anyway.

**Conclusions.** A $0.75 message to the top 5% pays for itself only if it keeps at least {{ results.fin.break_nudge }} of would-be churners; a $20 credit would need {{ results.fin.break_incent }}. The model measures risk, not response, so we recommend a capped randomised pilot and a stop rule rather than a rollout. All numbers are regenerated from the supplied data by the accompanying code.
